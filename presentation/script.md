## Slide 6. Four changes neuron morphology forced

*Target: 2 min 00 s. Source: `06-what-morphology-forced.md`*

**On screen:** Split branches into linear chains · Two-resolution crops: a neurite is about 3 px wide · Negative prompts on touching neighbours · Automated QC and triage, not hand proofreading

Opening: "Four changes, and each one is a problem followed by a fix."

Key points, one sentence each:
1. Branches. SAM2's video memory tracks a single object, and at a branch point it just picks one
   arm and follows it. So a branching neuron cannot propagate as one object. We walk the skeleton
   tree and cut it into maximal linear chains, propagate each chain separately, then recomposite
   into the per-neuron volume. That is the figure on the right: one seed per chain, propagating
   both ways.
2. Resolution. Propagation runs at an eightfold downscale because a 9000 by 9000 frame at native
   resolution exhausts a normal GPU. At that scale a neurite is about three pixels across, which
   is not enough for the model to find an edge. So the anchor is predicted on a higher-resolution
   crop around the skeleton node, and the resulting box is mapped back down to seed propagation.
3. Neighbours. Because cells touch, masks bleed into the cell next door. We seed negative prompts
   on the neighbouring skeleton nodes, which tells the model explicitly what is not the target.
4. Proofreading. The liver path proofreads by hand. At our scale that is the five-month number
   from earlier, so QC has to be automatic and only the flagged frames reach a human.

If time is short: this slide and the next can be merged. This is the core slide, do not rush it.

## Slide 7. Re-anchoring every slice beats propagating memory

*Target: 1 min 30 s. Source: `07-two-ways-to-run.md`*

**On screen:** Propagation is smooth, but memory drifts onto the wrong cell · Per-slice re-seeds from its own node: dropout 0.129 to 0.001 · The cost is rare blow-ups, caught by a guard

Opening: "There are two ways to run this, and the fork matters more than anything else in the
pipeline."

Key points:
- Video propagation is the original recipe. It is temporally smooth, which is what you want for a
  3D mesh, but the memory accumulates error: after enough slices it is confidently tracking the
  neighbouring cell, or it has lost the cell entirely.
- Per-slice re-anchoring throws the memory away and re-seeds every single slice from that slice's
  own skeleton node. Drift becomes impossible by construction. Measured: the rate of frames where
  we lose our own cell drops from 0.129 to 0.001.
- The cost is on screen. Occasionally a per-slice prediction escapes and takes the entire animal,
  the three red frames. Those are trivially detectable, they are three orders of magnitude too
  big, so a blow-up guard catches them. Note the areas: about 400 pixels normally, 811,163 on the
  blow-up.
- So per-slice trades a slow systematic failure for a rare obvious one. Obvious failures are much
  better, because we can catch them automatically.

Transition: "Now, how do I know any of that? None of those numbers exist without a ruler."

## Slide 8. A ground-truth-free ruler, built from the skeletons

*Target: 2 min 00 s. Source: `08-how-we-measure.md`*

**On screen:** Foreign node inside a mask means bleed · Own node missing means dropout · Measure, do not trust how it looks

Opening: "We have no ground truth on this worm. Nobody has hand-segmented it, that is the entire
point of the project. So we built a ruler out of the one thing we do have: the skeletons."

Key points:
- The rule is on screen and it is deliberately simple. Take a mask. It should contain its own
  neuron's centreline node, and it should contain nobody else's. If another neuron's node is
  inside the mask, the mask has reached the middle of the cell next door, so it has merged. If its
  own node is missing, we lost the cell.
- This costs nothing, needs no annotation, and it works on every frame of every chain.
- Be clear about the limits: it is a floor, not a full boundary metric. It only fires once a mask
  reaches a neighbour's centreline, so a mask that crosses a real membrane but stops short is
  invisible to it. We added a Sato ridge membrane map to catch that subtler case, which is coming
  up in two slides.
- The reason this slide exists in a progress talk: it caught real errors that the QC flags were
  completely blind to. The flags had no bleed signal at all, so for a while we were grading
  ourselves on the wrong thing. This is the "measure, do not vibe" lesson.
- Flag this limitation, because it is one of my questions later: this metric is per-frame, so it
  says nothing about whether a mask is consistent from slice to slice. That biases it toward
  per-slice and against propagation, and for 3D meshes temporal consistency matters.

## Slide 10. Overlaps resolve by score, or by real membranes

*Target: 1 min 30 s. Source: `10-resolving-overlaps.md`*

**On screen:** argmax: the highest-scoring mask wins the pixel · watershed: grow each mask out to the ridge walls · Magenta marks where the two disagree

Opening: "Every mask is predicted independently, so nothing stops two of them from claiming the
same pixel. On the left, white is where five masks overlap."

Key points:
- Option one, argmax: whichever mask scores highest takes the pixel. It is cheap, it always
  produces a decision, and the boundary it draws is arbitrary. It has no idea where the cell
  actually ends.
- Option two, watershed on the ridge map from the previous slide: treat the membranes as
  watersheds and let each mask grow until it hits a wall. The split then lands on a real membrane
  instead of an arbitrary line.
- Magenta is where the two methods disagree, so it is a direct picture of what the ridge map buys
  you. Look at the bottom right: argmax cuts through the middle of a cell, watershed follows the
  boundary.
- Status: argmax is built and running. Watershed is the prototype. This is a direction rather than
  a finished result, and it is one of the things I would like advice on, because it is also the
  natural place to plug in a trained membrane predictor instead of the Sato filter.

## Slide 13. The ruler passes two of these three

*Target: 2 min 00 s. Source: `13-where-it-breaks.md`*

**On screen:** SAM finds a closed boundary, not always the cell's · Nucleus capture and fragments score as clean · Branch points still split one neuron into many chains

Opening: "Here is the honest part. These are three real masks from the current best
configuration, and two of them are scored as perfect."

Key points:
- Left, nucleus capture. The mask fills the nucleus and stops at the nuclear envelope, so there is
  a rim of cytoplasm and the actual cell membrane sitting outside it. This is the most common
  failure on somas, and the reason is structural: the nuclear envelope is a stronger, cleaner,
  more closed boundary than the faint outer membrane. SAM is doing exactly what it was trained to
  do, it finds the most convincing object under the point. It just is not the cell.
- Middle, a fragment. The mask is a speck at the skeleton node inside a cell that is clearly much
  larger. The model found something, gave up early, and the own node is inside it, so it passes.
- Right, wrong object entirely: the mask locked onto a round organelle next to the node. This one
  the metric does catch, as a dropout, because the own node ends up outside the mask.
- So the unifying diagnosis is one sentence: SAM finds a closed boundary, and inside a cell there
  are several, of which the cell membrane is the faintest.
- Important consequence, and it is why this slide sits after the results: growing masks outward to
  the nearest membrane does NOT fix nucleus capture. The flood is trapped by the nuclear envelope.
  It needs either detection (spot that this is a nucleus) or a model that knows what a neuron is.
- Two of three being invisible to the metric is the strongest argument for question 3 on the next
  slide. My numbers improved, and they are also blind in a specific, nameable way.

Transition: "Which brings me to what I actually want from you."

## Slide 17 (backup). Backup: aligning the ground-truth worm

*On demand, for questions. Source: `17-backup-registration.md`*

**On screen:** The scored worm and the ground-truth worm are different animals · One global affine left a radial residual at the frame edge · Per-section fit: median residual 19.6 px to 4.7 px · Nodes landing on the right mask: 67.9% to 85.7%

This is the answer to "how do you validate against real ground truth".

The ground truth we have, SEM-Dauer 1 exported from VAST, is a different animal from the one we
segment. So any comparison needs the two put into a common frame. A single global affine was not
enough: it left a residual that grew radially toward the frame edge, tens of pixels out there.
Fitting per section brought the median residual from 19.6 to 4.7 pixels, and the fraction of
skeleton nodes landing inside the correct ground-truth mask from 67.9 to 85.7 percent.

Two caveats, and they are the reason the GT-free metric exists:
- 85.7 percent is not good enough to grade boundaries on. A boundary error and a registration error
  look identical.
- Our copy of the cross-worm segmentation is incomplete, and its neighbouring masks are inset from
  the shared membrane by design, so boundary metrics against it are biased regardless of alignment.

So cross-worm ground truth is a sanity check, not the ruler. The skeleton merge metric needs no
registration, which is precisely why it is the primary measure.

## Slide 18 (backup). Backup: the membrane map and its threshold

*On demand, for questions. Source: `18-backup-membrane-map.md`*

This is the Sato ridge map at whole-frame scale, plus what happens as the threshold tau moves.

Why it exists: the skeleton merge metric only fires once a mask reaches a neighbour's centreline,
so it cannot see a mask that crosses a real membrane and stops short. The membrane map gives three
extra per-frame signals: does a membrane ridge cut the mask border to border (mild bleed), what
fraction of the mask perimeter sits on a membrane, and how much room the mask left before its
enclosing membrane (underfill).

Two limitations:
- The v1 map traces real cell boundaries, but it also fires on mitochondria and vesicles. It is a
  comparative signal, not an absolute membrane detector. The interface is deliberately swappable,
  so a trained membrane model can sit behind it later without changing anything downstream.
- On a soma, the nuclear envelope is a STRONGER ridge than the faint outer cell membrane. That is
  the nucleus-capture problem seen from the membrane side, and it is why growing a mask outward to
  the nearest membrane does not fix nucleus capture: the flood is trapped by the nuclear envelope.

Tau is a specificity knob, not a correctness knob. Higher tau keeps only the strongest ridges.

## Slide 20 (backup). Backup: how branches become linear chains

*On demand, for questions. Source: `20-backup-branch-handling.md`*

The detail behind change 1 on slide 6.

Two preprocessing steps run before any segmentation. First, virtual nodes are inserted so the
centreline is sampled densely enough in z, bridging gaps up to a set allowance. Second, a
depth-first walk over the skeleton tree splits it into maximal linear chains. A branch is a node
with several children, or a merge where several parents converge on one child.

Why it is necessary: SAM2's video memory tracks a single object. At a branch it keeps following one
arm, so a whole branching neuron cannot propagate as one object. Each chain propagates separately
and the results are recomposited into the per-neuron volume.

Known limitation, and it is one of the three open failure modes: the branch rule is a heuristic. It
is right for the large majority of cases but it does not catch every one, and the recomposition step
is where two chains of the same neuron can disagree with each other about the same pixel.

The liver pipeline never needs this step, because each of its structures is one compact object.
